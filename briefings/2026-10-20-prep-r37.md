- **Claimed results:** autonomous coding accuracy up to 97% for some service lines (ER, physician office visits). Vendor-reported; measurement method not public.
- **Scale (from the JD):** 95 of the top 100 US health systems, 180M+ claims, 550M+ patient encounters, 1.2B+ workflow actions a year.
- **Recent (from Amber):** University of Florida program; Riyadh, Saudi Arabia project needing ~400 hires.

### 3 product areas to have an opinion on
1. **Which coding goes autonomous and which goes to a coder.**
   *Your take:* Tier autonomy by confidence *and* dollar impact, not just by service line. ER and office visits can go fully autonomous at 97%; inpatient DRG coding, where one code swings reimbursement, needs coder review above a dollar or uncertainty threshold. Measure accuracy weighted by financial impact and audit defensibility, not just exact-match rate. This is your K3 tiered-autonomy pattern.
2. **One platform vs. point products** (coding, CDI, prior auth, denials).
   *Your take:* The durable asset is the shared layer: clinical evidence extraction plus payer logic (Payer Atlas). Each workflow should be a thin product on top. This is what you did at Keystone, turning bespoke client work into a reusable platform (K1).
3. **Pre-bill vs. post-bill.**
   *Your take:* Shift left. Every denial prevented pre-bill is cheaper than one appealed later. Define success with business metrics (denial rate, clean-claim rate, days in A/R), model metrics (accuracy, coverage, auto-rate), and adoption metrics (coder override and edit rates). The JD asks for exactly these three metric types.

### Domain vocabulary to learn before the round
Revenue cycle: patient access (eligibility, prior authorization) → charge capture → clinical documentation → coding (ICD-10-CM/PCS, CPT, DRGs for inpatient) → claim submission → denials and appeals → payment posting and collections. Also know CDI, DNFB (discharged not final billed), clean-claim rate, first-pass yield, and why audits matter (overcoding is a compliance risk, undercoding loses revenue). You don't need to be an expert; you need to show you've done the work.
